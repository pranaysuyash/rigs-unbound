# Stranger at the Silo — Opening Narrative Exploration

- Date: 2026-07-29
- Status: **Idea / proposed exploration; not accepted canon**
- Evidence tier: Tier 0–1 (operator seed plus static comparison with current
  Farmfall, restoration, and episode documents)
- Owner / next reviewer: Project owner

## The seed, preserved

The operator's original idea:

> a stranger reaches a far out remote farm while lost, the owner a very old man,
> when the stranger asks for help he's told if he fixes the old man's
> dilapidated old tractor which he shows lying around the back of the silo all
> broken etc. he might be able to help ...the stranger agrees because he's
> looking for a place to stay and food etc.

This artifact expands the seed without promoting its unresolved details into
project canon.

## Working thesis

The game should begin with a person arriving at the edge of a life already in
progress, then earning shelter by bringing a neglected machine back into the
world. The first meaningful relationship is not a dialogue tree or a quest
marker. It is the relationship between the stranger, the old keeper, the farm,
and the tractor that gradually becomes useful again.

The tractor is not a prop the stranger repairs before the “real game” starts.
Repairing it is the first act of the game, and driving it is the first proof
that the stranger has earned a place in this world.

## Why this belongs in Rigs Unbound

The premise extends existing direction rather than creating a separate story
layer:

- **ADR-0002** already proposes arrival at a damaged tractor and neglected
  field around a workshop/silo as the first playable trigger.
- **Tractor Restoration and Modular Growth** defines the machine journey as
  `found → stabilized → working → specialized → hybridized → storied`.
- **Farmfall** already gives the first location a day/night/dawn consequence
  shape.
- The **episode grammar** says that a persistent vehicle, place, pressure,
  discovery, and consequence should compose into one episode rather than a set
  of unrelated modes.
- The recent **Missing Middle** exploration identifies a current experience
  gap: the build can feel like departure followed by arrival, without enough
  lived journey between them.

This opening supplies an authored emotional bridge into those systems. It gives
the player a reason to remain, a human-scale first contract, and a believable
way to encounter the machine before the wider world opens.

## Recommended opening shape

### 1. Arrival — the player is already in trouble

The stranger reaches the farm near late afternoon, exhausted, hungry, and
uncertain whether the track they followed is even a public road. The farm is
not presented as a clean hub: the silo leans, the yard is overgrown, the house
is maintained only where necessary, and the old tractor sits behind the silo
under a collapsed lean-to.

The player should be able to move and look immediately. The opening does not
need a long cinematic. The farm, the old man, and the tractor should answer the
first three questions visually:

1. Where am I?
2. Who might help me?
3. What can I do right now?

### 2. The bargain — shelter in exchange for usefulness

The stranger asks for directions, food, or a place to sleep. The old man does
not offer a free exposition scene. He offers terms:

> “You want the road, you’ll need to make that thing move.”

The old man can provide a meal and a bedroll, but only after the stranger
proves they are not another person who takes from the farm and leaves its
problems behind. This keeps the relationship practical rather than instantly
sentimental.

The bargain needs a concrete promise. The recommended version is:

> If the tractor can move before morning, the old man can take the stranger to
> a high ridge or radio relay where they can find the road out.

That promise is useful even if it later becomes complicated or incomplete. The
stranger has a credible reason to stay, and the old man has a credible reason
to care whether the tractor works.

### 3. Inspection — the tractor tells its own story

The old man shows the machine rather than describing its whole history. The
player finds:

- a seized or unreliable engine;
- a missing belt, hose, battery, or starter component;
- a damaged front mount where the signature tool used to sit;
- one repair that is obviously recent and one that is decades old;
- a cab, plate, patch, or scar that makes the tractor recognizable before it is
  functional.

The old man knows where some parts are, but not all of them. His knowledge is
fragmentary because the farm has been isolated for a long time. This makes the
first repair a small exploration of the yard and nearby field, not a menu
interaction.

### 4. First work — the player learns the farm by fixing it

The first task should be a compact chain of physical actions:

`inspect → find usable part → clean/fit part → attempt start → diagnose next fault`

The player may find a second part in an ordinary place: an old pump shed, a
discarded implement, a shallow ditch, or the back of the silo. The objects
should make the farm legible. The player learns where things are by paying
attention, not by following floating objectives through an empty map.

The old man can help through short, conditional observations:

- “That belt won’t hold. There’s another one on the grain auger.”
- “Don’t force the starter. It’s been waiting longer than you have.”
- “If you get it rolling, keep clear of the north field after dark.”

The third line is the first hint that the farm is more than a realistic repair
sim, but the opening should earn that mystery through ordinary detail first.

### 5. First movement — the tractor becomes the player's body

When the tractor starts, the game should not immediately send the player away.
The first drive should be a short, consequential test around the farm:

- tow a stuck implement clear of the silo;
- carry a part or water tank to the workshop;
- pull open a blocked farm gate;
- grade or plough one damaged strip so the route becomes easier to use.

This is where the premise becomes distinctly Rigs Unbound. The player does not
just receive a repaired stat block. They feel the machine's weight, turning
radius, traction, noise, lights, and limitations. The farm begins to remember
the work through ruts, cleared space, moved equipment, and the tractor's
visible condition.

### 6. Dusk — the promise becomes a choice

The old man originally said the tractor must move before morning, but the
player's first successful drive happens as light is leaving the valley. The
stranger can:

- stop, eat, and sleep at the farm;
- attempt one more repair before night;
- follow the old man's warning and investigate the north field;
- take the tractor toward the relay and risk leaving before it is fully ready.

The recommended first slice does not offer four equal branches. It offers one
clear safe option and one tempting risk. The player should feel that they chose
to stay involved in the farm rather than being held by an invisible timer.

This maps directly onto Farmfall's proposed day → gloam → night → dawn loop.
The first night is not a generic combat tutorial. It is the first time the
player sees what the old man was protecting and why the tractor's lights,
engine noise, tool posture, and physical mass matter.

### 7. The morning after — help is earned, not guaranteed

At dawn, the old man should honor the bargain in some form, but not necessarily
by immediately pointing to an exit. Possible outcomes:

- he opens an old route and gives the player a hand-drawn map;
- he reveals that the nearest road is gone and the tractor is now the only
  practical way out;
- he asks the stranger to deliver something to the relay before he explains
  the safest route;
- he recognizes that the tractor has chosen a new keeper and offers a larger
  arrangement: stay, work, and learn what happened to the valley.

The strongest long-term version is **partial truth**: the old man genuinely can
help, but he has withheld the fact that leaving safely requires the farm and the
tractor to be made functional together. He is not a villain; he is someone who
has survived by giving away only the information another person has earned.

## The old man's function

The old man should not become a conventional quest-giver who dispenses a chain
of errands. He has four better functions:

1. **Gatekeeper of local knowledge** — he knows the land, weather, old routes,
   and danger signs.
2. **Witness to machine history** — he can identify what each repair means,
   without explaining everything upfront.
3. **Moral pressure** — he gives the player a reason to care about consequences
   beyond scrap and upgrades.
4. **A possible future** — he shows what happens when a person stays with one
   machine and one place long enough for both to become storied.

He should remain capable and observant despite his age. If he is helpless in
every respect, the bargain feels artificial. He can still mend wire, cook,
read weather, remember routes, and make hard judgments; the tractor is the
thing his body can no longer safely operate or recover.

## What is the stranger's role?

This is a key design boundary.

The stranger is useful as a framing presence because they give the player a
reason to arrive and a reason to ask questions. But the stranger must not
become a fully authored human hero whose story crowds out the vehicle. The
player's identity should remain open enough for the tractor to become the
primary character.

Recommended boundary:

- The stranger has a concrete immediate need but no fixed biography in the
  opening.
- The game uses second-person or lightly voiced dialogue rather than defining
  the player's personality.
- The old man reacts to what the player does, not to a prewritten destiny.
- The tractor's repairs, scars, attachments, and choices become the more
  specific record of who the player is.

The stranger is the doorway. The machine is the protagonist.

## Three mystery directions

### A. The practical road mystery — recommended baseline

The old man needs the tractor to reopen or reach a route to a radio relay. The
valley has been cut off by weather, collapse, or a failed bridge. The mystery
is initially human and geographical, then widens as the player discovers that
the route has not failed in an ordinary way.

**Strength:** believable motivation, easy first gameplay, preserves player
agency, and supports a gradual escalation.

**Risk:** if the reveal never becomes stranger, the opening may remain a
beautiful but conventional repair story.

### B. The buried signal — strongest Farmfall connection

The tractor is needed to clear or plough a strip beneath which an old signal,
beacon, or machine has been buried. The old man knows the signal exists but
does not know what still answers it. Night threats react to its activation.

**Strength:** connects restoration, land memory, signature ecology, and the
day/night loop through one physical action.

**Risk:** too much mystery too early can make the farm feel like a disguised
quest hub rather than a lived place.

### C. The inherited machine — strongest emotional version

The tractor belonged to someone the old man lost. He has kept it intact enough
to remember but not functional enough to use. The stranger's repair work forces
him to decide whether he wants the machine restored or only preserved.

**Strength:** gives repairs emotional weight and makes scars/history central.

**Risk:** can become sentimental or make the player feel like a tool in the old
man's grief.

### Recommendation

Use **A as the surface**, **B as the first escalation**, and **C as the human
subtext**. The player first believes they are fixing a route, then discovers
the land is answering the machine, while the old man's guarded behavior hints
at why he stopped using the tractor in the first place.

## Dialogue texture

Dialogue should be sparse, specific, and slightly withholding. The old man
should make the player curious without sounding like a lore delivery system.

Possible opening exchange:

> **Stranger:** “Is there a road through here?”
>
> **Old man:** “There was.”
>
> **Stranger:** “Can you help me?”
>
> **Old man:** “I can feed you. I can give you a dry place until morning. If
> you want the road, make the tractor move.”
>
> **Stranger:** “That thing?”
>
> **Old man:** “That thing has taken people farther than you’ve walked.”

The final line carries the right ambiguity: it can sound like ordinary farm
history before it becomes something more.

## Gameplay and story crosswalk

| Narrative beat | Player action | Persistent meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stranger reaches farm | Explore yard and approach old man | Farm becomes the first known place |
| Bargain is offered | Accept work for food/shelter | Favor begins as a relationship, not a currency prompt |
| Tractor is inspected | Read damage, sockets, and scars | Vehicle history is legible before progression |
| Parts are found | Search adjacent farm spaces | Spatial discovery teaches the local geography |
| First repair succeeds | Start and move tractor | Restoration changes the player's body and options |
| Yard problem is solved | Tow, clear, grade, or plough | The world visibly remembers useful work |
| Dusk arrives | Rest or risk another action | Time is a choice with consequence, not a punishment timer |
| Night pressure appears | Use light, mass, tool, route, or avoidance | The same machine verb crosses into danger |
| Dawn arrives | Review damage, route, and old man's response | Consequence becomes relationship and next possibility |

## What the first playable should prove

This opening is worth prototyping only if it proves these player-facing claims:

1. A player understands why they are at the farm without reading a document.
2. A player understands why repairing the tractor is worth doing.
3. The tractor feels like a character before the wider fleet exists.
4. The old man creates curiosity without replacing exploration with exposition.
5. The first repair changes at least one real vehicle verb, not only a number.
6. The day/night transition is caused by player activity and readable pressure.
7. The player can describe one cause-and-effect story afterward: “I repaired
   X, which let me do Y, but it caused or revealed Z.”

## Kill test and failure modes

The premise should be rejected or reworked if:

- the stranger spends too long walking and the vehicle is not the first
  character the player remembers;
- the old man could give the same information without needing the tractor;
- repair becomes a checklist of arbitrary fetch items;
- the promised help is obviously fake, making the player feel manipulated;
- the farm is only a tutorial room that disappears once the tractor works;
- the mystery overwhelms the believable human bargain before attachment forms;
- the first night turns the tractor into a generic combat platform;
- the player is forced to obey a timer instead of choosing whether to risk dusk;
- the story fixes the stranger's biography so tightly that player-owned vehicle
  history becomes secondary.

The strongest argument against the idea is that “stranger helps old farmer” is
a familiar story shape. It survives that objection only if the tractor and
the farm are not scenery: the repair must create a new playable relationship,
and the first night must prove that the machine's ordinary capabilities have
unexpected consequences.

## Build conditions

### Proceed as an authored opening experiment if

- the first playable can reach the farm, bargain, inspect the tractor, and
  attempt a repair without a long cinematic;
- the old man's help promise is concrete enough to motivate action;
- at least one repair part is found through spatial attention;
- the first successful drive solves a local problem;
- the player's first night decision produces a visible dawn consequence.

### Prototype before making it the campaign opening if

- the player avatar's presence competes with vehicle identity;
- the old man's dialogue requires a large branching system;
- the opening needs a new save model, relationship authority, or NPC runtime;
- the tractor cannot be made drivable without adding a second representation of
  the vehicle;
- the reveal depends on bespoke cinematic tooling rather than the existing
  world and camera grammar.

### Do not do yet

- Do not add a new narrative runtime or dialogue engine for this idea.
- Do not create a second quest authority alongside the contract ledger and
  episode runner.
- Do not add a fixed player biography before the vehicle identity and opening
  flow have been tested.
- Do not treat this exploration as acceptance of a final story, NPC, or
  production asset.

## Open decisions for the project owner

1. Is the stranger an intentionally blank player frame, or should they have a
   visible vehicle/previous life on arrival?
2. What exact help does the old man promise: road, relay, map, transport, or
   knowledge about why the valley is isolated?
3. Does the tractor already start briefly, or is the first successful start the
   player's first major reward?
4. Is the old man the tractor's owner, its last keeper, or the person who has
   been protecting it from something?
5. Should the first night be optional risk, a consequence of staying too long,
   or the central reason the old man needs the tractor before dawn?
6. What part of the tractor's past should be visible before it is explained?
7. Does the stranger ultimately leave the farm, stay temporarily, or become
   the next keeper of the route?

## Decision posture

Keep this as a **proposed opening experiment** under Farmfall and the broader
Living Atlas direction. It is strong enough to guide a paper sequence and a
small playable opening test, but not strong enough to change ADR-0002, the
episode grammar, save state, NPC architecture, or campaign canon without
operator review and player evidence.

## Anything else?

Yes. The most important additional implication is that the opening can make
**Favor** emotionally legible before it becomes an economy concept. The player
does not first see a favor meter; they experience a person deciding whether to
trust the machine they have just repaired. If the idea survives prototyping,
that relationship should remain a world-facing consequence of useful work,
not be flattened into a generic quest reward.
